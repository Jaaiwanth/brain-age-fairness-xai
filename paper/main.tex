\begin{abstract}
Brain-age models predict chronological age from structural MRI, and the brain-age gap is studied as a marker of brain health. When such a model shows a disparity between acquisition sites, the disparity is often attributed to scanner differences without a test. We present a layered, pre-specified audit of a UK Biobank-pretrained SFCN fine-tuned on 485 T1-weighted IXI scans from three London sites. The model is accurate (test MAE 3.71 years, $r=0.966$) and its errors and brain-age gaps are equivalent between sexes within $\pm2$ years. Scans from one site (IOP) receive a brain-age gap about 4--5 years lower than the reference site. The offset replicates across splits and in 5-fold cross-validation over all 412 non-test subjects ($-5.24$ years, 95\% CI $-7.62$ to $-2.87$, 52 IOP subjects), survives eight ways of handling age, including age matching and a nonlinear adjustment, and is already present in the pretrained model before any training on IXI ($-3.3$ years, $p=5\times10^{-6}$). The model is sensitive to acquisition appearance: the site is linearly decodable from its features (91\% vs a 50\% permutation ceiling), and giving Hammersmith brains IOP's intensity appearance lowers their predicted age by 3.9 years. Yet this sensitivity does not explain the disparity: intensity, matched image smoothness, head size, education, BMI, ethnicity, and removing site information from the features with ComBat, ComBat-GAM or age-conditional concept erasure all leave the offset in place. Naive concept erasure appears to remove it only by also erasing age information. Attribution maps pass randomisation and deletion tests under two baselines. The model's own 90\% ranges cover only 77\% of out-of-sample IOP subjects, and pooled conformal intervals 69\%; per-site conformal intervals restore valid coverage at every site. We summarise the audit as a protocol-based fairness passport with graded, pre-specified verdicts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================
Brain-age models learn to predict a person's chronological age from a structural MRI scan \cite{cole2017trends,franke2019ten}. The brain-age gap (BAG) between predicted and chronological age is interpreted as accelerated or delayed brain ageing and has been associated with mortality and neurological disease \cite{cole2018mortality}. Deep convolutional networks predict age with errors of a few years \cite{cole2017deep,peng2021sfcn,bashyam2020dbn,leonardsen2022pyment}.

Accuracy alone does not make a model trustworthy. A model can be accurate on average and biased for a subgroup \cite{larrazabal2020gender,seyyed2021underdiagnosis,riccilara2022fairness,picarra2023brainage}; it can exploit non-biological shortcuts such as hospital-specific acquisition properties \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,zech2018pneumonia,degrave2021covid}; and the explanations used to inspect it can themselves be unfaithful \cite{adebayo2018sanity,hooker2019benchmark}. In multi-site MRI, scanner differences are a well-known source of site effects \cite{glocker2019scanner,fortin2018harmonization,dinsdale2021unlearning}, and differences in brain-age gap between datasets can largely reflect their age distributions \cite{aguzin2026bias}.

These concerns meet in a common situation: a brain-age model shows a disparity between sites, and the disparity is attributed to the scanner. That attribution is rarely tested. We audit a widely used network on the public IXI dataset and treat ``scanner bias'' as a hypothesis to be tested rather than assumed. Our narrative follows the evidence: (1) the model is accurate; (2) it shows a replicated site disparity that is not an artefact of age handling and exists before fine-tuning; (3) the model is sensitive to acquisition appearance; (4) but that sensitivity does not explain the disparity; (5) a disparity should therefore not be labelled scanner bias without such tests.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A pre-specified, layered audit protocol (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}) with graded verdicts based on equivalence tests, minimum detectable effects and replication, released as a reusable passport schema in the spirit of model cards \cite{mitchell2019modelcards}.
\item A site disparity shown to be robust to eight age-handling choices, to cross-validation over all non-test subjects and to independent training runs, and present in the pretrained model before any exposure to IXI.
\item Direct evidence of acquisition sensitivity (site decodability against a permutation null; an age- and sex-standardised intensity-swap stress test), and a systematic rule-out showing that neither acquisition properties we can measure nor recorded cohort characteristics explain the disparity.
\item A methodological warning: concept erasure that ignores the age--site confound appears to remove the disparity by removing age information.
\item Validated explanations and uncertainty: attribution maps tested under two deletion baselines and against Integrated Gradients, and conformal intervals with per-site (Mondrian) coverage.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{Brain-age prediction.} Deep 3D CNNs trained on raw images \cite{cole2017deep}, DeepBrainNet \cite{bashyam2020dbn}, the lightweight SFCN \cite{peng2021sfcn} and its multi-cohort descendants \cite{leonardsen2022pyment} predict age within a few years. Brain-age estimates regress towards the mean age, so a bias correction is standard; whether to correct the prediction or include age as a covariate remains debated \cite{le2018nonlinear,smith2019delta,delange2020correction}.

\textbf{Fairness and shortcuts in medical imaging.} Imbalanced data produce biased classifiers \cite{larrazabal2020gender} and under-served groups are underdiagnosed \cite{seyyed2021underdiagnosis}; see \cite{riccilara2022fairness}. For brain age, race and sex bias was analysed in UK Biobank \cite{picarra2023brainage}, and a multi-cohort evaluation found dataset differences largely explained by age distributions, with a small ethnicity effect in some models \cite{aguzin2026bias}. Models encode protected attributes they were not trained on \cite{glocker2023encoding}, measurable with linear probes \cite{alain2016probes}; decodability does not imply use, which motivates erasure-based tests \cite{belrose2023leace}.

\textbf{Site effects and harmonisation.} Scanner signatures are learnable \cite{glocker2019scanner}. ComBat \cite{johnson2007combat,fortin2018harmonization} and its nonlinear-age extension ComBat-GAM \cite{pomponio2020combatgam} remove site effects from derived measures; adversarial unlearning removes them during training \cite{ganin2016dann,dinsdale2021unlearning}; histogram matching standardises intensity \cite{nyul2000standardization}.

\textbf{Faithful explanations and uncertainty.} Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}, HiResCAM \cite{draelos2020hirescam} and Integrated Gradients \cite{sundararajan2017ig} are common attribution methods; they should pass randomisation \cite{adebayo2018sanity} and perturbation tests \cite{samek2017evaluating,hooker2019benchmark}. Network confidence is often miscalibrated \cite{guo2017calibration}; deep ensembles \cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} and conformal prediction \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023conformal}, including group-conditional (Mondrian) variants, provide checked or guaranteed coverage.

\section{Data}
%=====================================================================
IXI \cite{ixi} provides T1-weighted MRI and demographics for healthy adults scanned at Guy's Hospital (Guys; Philips 1.5\,T), Hammersmith Hospital (HH; Philips 3\,T) and the Institute of Psychiatry (IOP; GE 1.5\,T). Labels were rebuilt from the official spreadsheet after an identifier error in an early table was found; two independent rebuilds agreed, and no result based on the erroneous labels was reported. Of 502 subjects with a scan and a demographic index entry, 17 were excluded (10 without a record, 2 without age, 2 with contradictory duplicates, and 3 with clean labels that were not part of the shared team split; the list is released). The remaining 485 were split 339/73/73 into train, validation and test, stratified by site (Table~\ref{tab:data}). IOP recruited younger participants (Fig.~\ref{fig:agedist}), so every group comparison adjusts for age, and Section~\ref{sec:agerob} tests whether that adjustment is adequate.

\section{Methods}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Preprocessing and model}
Each scan underwent N4 bias-field correction \cite{tustison2010n4}, skull-stripping with ANTsPyNet \cite{tustison2021antsx}, affine registration to the MNI152NLin6Asym 1\,mm template \cite{ciric2022templateflow}, a centre crop to $160\times192\times160$ voxels and SFCN's intensity normalisation (division by the mean over the full $182\times218\times182$ box). SFCN \cite{peng2021sfcn} has five convolution--batch-norm--max-pool--ReLU blocks and a $1\times1\times1$ block, global average pooling to 64 features and a $1\times1\times1$ convolution over one-year age bins; the prediction is the expected value of the bin distribution. We replaced the UK Biobank head (ages 42--82) with 72 bins for 18--90, warm-starting overlapping bins, and fine-tuned with Gaussian soft labels ($\sigma=2$ years) and a KL loss: the head for 5 epochs (learning rate $10^{-3}$), then the whole network with AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} (head $10^{-3}$, backbone $10^{-4}$), early stopping on validation MAE (patience 15). The checkpoint at epoch 30 was frozen. Bias correction followed \cite{cole2018mortality}: $\hat{y}=a\,y+b$ fitted on validation ($a=0.832$, $b=8.30$), corrected prediction $(\hat{y}-b)/a$.

\textbf{Cross-validated replication.} To estimate group effects on more than 18 held-out IOP subjects without touching the test set, the 412 train+validation subjects were split into 5 site-stratified folds; each fold was predicted by a model trained on the other four (85\% training, 15\% inner validation for early stopping and for that fold's bias correction). Full fine-tuning was infeasible on our CPU hardware, so the first three blocks were kept at the UK Biobank weights, which never saw IXI, and the remaining blocks and head were trained with the same objective and schedule. Every non-test subject thus receives an out-of-sample prediction (52 IOP). Training was repeated with \pend{n} seeds, and the fold models' average forms an ensemble for the locked test set.

\subsection{Analysis plan}
Fixed before the test set was evaluated: the split, the exclusions, the bias-correction procedure, the attribution layer and method, and the regression model for group effects. Fixed before the cross-validation results were seen (and committed to the public repository with a timestamp): the equivalence margin, the graded verdict rules and the passport criteria below. The remaining analyses are tests of explanations for an effect already observed and are reported as such.

\subsection{Group fairness and graded verdicts}
Group effects were estimated with $\mathrm{outcome}\sim\mathrm{sex}+\mathrm{site}+\mathrm{age}$ (OLS, HC3 errors \cite{mackinnon1985hc}) for corrected BAG and absolute error, with bootstrap CIs \cite{efron1993bootstrap}. Each effect received a pre-specified verdict with margin $\pm2$ years (about half the MAE): \textsc{pass} if equivalence is shown by two one-sided tests \cite{schuirmann1987tost} (90\% CI within the margin); \textsc{inconclusive} if the 95\% CI includes zero without equivalence; \textsc{concern} if it excludes zero but the effect is within the margin or does not replicate; \textsc{fail} if it excludes zero, exceeds the margin and replicates in an independent split. We report the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power and Holm-adjusted \cite{holm1979} $p$-values across all group comparisons. Leave-one-site-out models (trained without one site) measured generalisation to an unseen site.

\subsection{Robustness of the site effect to age}
\label{sec:agemethods}
The IOP-vs-Guys effect was re-estimated under: (a) no bias correction; (b) correction fitted on validation; (c) correction fitted on train; (d) age as a covariate of the raw prediction \cite{le2018nonlinear}; (e) a B-spline of age (4 df); (f) Guys and HH restricted to IOP's age range; (g) 1:1 age- and sex-matched IOP--Guys pairs (caliper 3 years); and (h) native brain volume added as a covariate, because registration removes head size from the input. We also inspected gaps of the youngest subjects, near the lower edge of the label range.

\subsection{Is the offset learned?}
The UK Biobank model never saw IXI, so its predictions for all subjects aged 42--82 (its output range) show whether the offset exists before fine-tuning. In the cross-validation runs, the gaps of training and out-of-sample IOP subjects were recorded at every epoch.

\subsection{Acquisition sensitivity}
\textbf{Site probe.} A class-balanced multinomial logistic regression \cite{alain2016probes} was trained on train subjects' 64-d features with age, age$^2$ and sex regressed out and scored on held-out subjects (balanced accuracy), against a null from 30 label permutations.

\textbf{Stress test.} Each held-out brain's intensity distribution was quantile-matched \cite{nyul2000standardization} to each site's reference and re-predicted. References came from train subjects and were standardised to a common age and sex mix (per-site regression of the quantile vector on age, age$^2$ and sex, evaluated at the pooled mean), because tissue contrast itself carries age information. Effects are relative to matching to the subject's own site. Contrast, noise, blur and non-uniformity were also varied singly on 40 subjects. The test was repeated with the cross-validated models.

\subsection{Explaining the disparity}
\textbf{Image properties.} We compared native resolution and three sharpness measures (gradient, Laplacian, smoothness FWHM \cite{forman1995fwhm}) across sites, and blurred each held-out Guys and HH brain to IOP's adjusted smoothness.

\textbf{Removing site from the features.} Site was removed from the 64-d features, with parameters from train only, by ComBat \cite{johnson2007combat,fortin2018harmonization} (linear age, age$^2$, sex preserved), ComBat-GAM-style harmonisation \cite{pomponio2020combatgam} (spline age), and LEACE concept erasure \cite{belrose2023leace}, both plain and conditional (erasing site only from the part of the features not explained by age and sex). Age was re-predicted through the model's own head, which reproduces the model exactly on the original features.

\textbf{Cohort characteristics.} IXI records education, ethnicity, height and weight. We compared sites on university degree, ethnicity and BMI and added them as covariates to the site model, using the zero-shot, main and cross-validated predictions. We also report the ethnicity effect itself.

\subsection{Attribution and its validity}
Standard Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} produced empty maps for many subjects, so we used location-wise gradient $\times$ activation (HiResCAM-style \cite{draelos2020hirescam}) at block \texttt{conv\_2} (8\,mm cells), chosen before any group comparison. Maps were summarised by Harvard--Oxford region \cite{jenkinson2012fsl}, both as shares and as density (share divided by the region's share of brain volume), with FDR correction \cite{benjamini1995fdr}. Validity: a cascading randomisation check \cite{adebayo2018sanity}; a deletion test \cite{samek2017evaluating,hooker2019benchmark} on 60 held-out subjects removing top-, bottom- or randomly-ranked 5/10\% of cells with two baselines (heavy smoothing, and the brain's mean intensity, which involves no blur), reporting signed and absolute changes; and agreement with Integrated Gradients \cite{sundararajan2017ig} (zero baseline, 16 steps) on 20 subjects.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
We assessed the model's own predictive distribution (coverage and width of its central 50/80/90/95\% ranges, per site), split-conformal intervals from out-of-fold absolute residuals \cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}, pooled and per site (Mondrian \cite{vovk2005algorithmic}), and the spread of the seed ensemble \cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}. Training with $\sigma=2$ soft labels sets a floor of about 2 years on the model's own spread.

\section{Results}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Accuracy}
The UK Biobank SFCN without fine-tuning had a validation MAE of 10.21 years ($r=0.910$), because its head cannot predict outside 42--82. Fine-tuning reduced it to 3.90. On the test set, evaluated once, the MAE was \textbf{3.71} years \ci{3.10}{4.38} and $r=0.966$ (Table~\ref{tab:acc}). The cross-validated models, which fine-tune only the upper blocks, reached an out-of-sample MAE of 4.76 years ($r=0.943$) over all 412 non-test subjects, and their ensemble 4.97 ($r=0.954$) on the locked test set. An independently fine-tuned SFCN and DeepBrainNet reached test MAE 4.07 and 4.53.

\subsection{Group fairness}
Table~\ref{tab:verdicts} lists every group effect with its verdict. On the test set alone, absolute error is \emph{equivalent} between sexes and between HH and Guys (\textsc{pass}), whereas the sex difference in BAG ($-0.59$, minimum detectable effect 3.1 years) is \textsc{inconclusive}: 73 test subjects cannot show equivalence. IOP scans receive a BAG 5.06 years lower than Guys \ci{-8.66}{-1.34}, replicated in validation ($-4.08$), giving \textsc{fail}. With all 412 cross-validated predictions the picture sharpens: the sex differences in BAG ($+0.61$, MDE 1.6) and in error ($+0.11$) are now \emph{equivalent} (\textsc{pass}); IOP's BAG offset is $-5.24$ \ci{-7.56}{-2.92} (Holm $p<0.001$, \textsc{fail}); HH shows a smaller offset within the margin ($-1.51$, \textsc{concern}); and errors are larger at HH ($+1.61$) and IOP ($+4.06$), which did not replicate on the locked test set (\textsc{concern}). A model never trained on IOP was 3.30 years \ci{1.62}{4.93} less accurate there, whereas one never trained on HH generalised (+0.25).

\subsection{The IOP offset is not an artefact of age handling}
\label{sec:agerob}
Across all eight age-handling choices, the held-out IOP offset ranged from $-3.11$ to $-4.53$ years, with every CI excluding zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:agerob}); on the test set alone it ranged from $-4.21$ to $-5.24$. It persisted in age-matched pairs ($-3.11$ \ci{-5.67}{-0.33}), with a spline for age ($-3.98$) and with no correction at all ($-3.77$). Native brain volume did not differ between IOP and Guys after age and sex adjustment ($-16$\,ml, $p=0.38$) and did not change the offset ($-4.47$). The offset is not at the edge of the label range: held-out IOP subjects under 30 had a mean gap of $+0.5$ years, those over 30 of $-5.6$ years. In the cross-validated predictions the offset was between $-3.94$ (no correction, and age as covariate) and $-5.24$ years across the choices, $-4.65$ with a spline for age and $-4.15$ \ci{-6.62}{-1.86} in 51 age- and sex-matched pairs, all with $p<0.001$.

\subsection{The offset exists before fine-tuning}
The UK Biobank model, which never saw an IXI scan, already predicts IOP brains \textbf{3.34 years younger} than Guys brains of the same age and sex \ci{-4.71}{-1.91} ($p=5\times10^{-6}$, 241 subjects aged 42--82, 19 IOP). Memorisation of IOP training subjects therefore cannot be its cause. Cross-validated training shows how fine-tuning interacts with it (Fig.~\ref{fig:dyn}): after the first epoch, training and out-of-sample IOP subjects both have an age-adjusted offset of about $-2.5$ to $-2.9$ years; once the upper blocks start training (stage 2) the two diverge, and by the end of training the offset of the IOP subjects the model trains on has been fitted away ($+0.19$), while that of unseen IOP subjects has grown to $-4.49$. Fine-tuning hides the offset for the subjects it sees rather than causing it, which explains why it is absent in the training set but present in every held-out split.

\subsection{The model is sensitive to acquisition appearance}
\textbf{Site is encoded.} The probe identified the site of held-out scans with \textbf{91.2\%} \ci{86.6}{95.2} balanced accuracy after removing age and sex, against a permutation null with mean 34\% and 95th percentile 50\% (Table~\ref{tab:probe}). This is expected: site is decodable with 99.6\% from input intensity quantiles alone and with 92.8\% from the pretrained network, so the features inherit an input-level signature; the question is whether the prediction uses it.

\subsection{But acquisition sensitivity does not explain the offset}
Table~\ref{tab:ruleout} collects every explanation tested. \emph{Intensity:} swapping between the Guys and IOP appearances moves predictions by at most about 1 year; without age standardisation of the references this effect was overstated ($-0.60/+1.60$), because IOP's younger recruitment leaked into its reference. \emph{Smoothness:} native resolution is identical across sites, yet IOP inputs are smoother (FWHM $+0.87$\,mm); blurring Guys and HH brains to IOP's smoothness did not lower their predictions. \emph{Removing site from the features:} ComBat erased the site signature (probe 36.3\%) with unchanged accuracy, but the offset remained ($-3.95$); with spline age it remained ($-6.80$); with age-conditional LEACE it remained ($-4.46$). Only \emph{plain} LEACE removed it ($+0.40$), and only because, IOP's training subjects being younger, it also erased age information correlated with site; the conditional version, which preserves age, does not. \emph{Cohort:} IOP did not differ from Guys in university degree (68\% vs 56\%; adjusted $p=0.56$), ethnicity ($p=0.64$) or BMI ($p=0.49$), and adjusting for all three left the offset unchanged in the zero-shot ($-2.80$ vs $-2.74$) and main models ($-5.26$ vs $-5.13$) and in cross-validation ($-5.04$ vs $-5.05$). Ethnicity itself showed at most a small effect: non-White participants had a gap $+1.43$ years higher in cross-validation (\ci{-0.02}{2.88}, $p=0.054$, 74 non-White), $+1.95$ in the main model's held-out subjects ($p=0.04$, 21 non-White) and none in the zero-shot model ($-0.18$), in line with the small ethnicity effects reported elsewhere \cite{aguzin2026bias}; with these numbers we report it as \textsc{inconclusive}.

\subsection{Where the model looks, and whether to trust it}
Attribution concentrates on deep grey matter around the ventricles, periventricular white matter and the cerebellum; normalised by volume, deep grey matter receives 3.2 times its volume share (Fig.~\ref{fig:attr}). The maps depend on the learned weights (randomisation $\rho=0.13$). Removing the top-ranked 5\% of cells changed predictions far more than random or bottom-ranked cells under both baselines (Table~\ref{tab:del}): $|\Delta\hat{y}|$ 4.83 vs 1.18 vs 0.46 years with smoothing and 6.49 vs 1.99 vs 0.97 with mean infill (top $>$ random in 95\% and 88\% of subjects, Wilcoxon $p<10^{-8}$; random $>$ bottom, $p<10^{-4}$). The signed change from removing top-ranked cells was positive (+1.67 and +2.56 years): predictions became \emph{older}, opposite to the effect of blur, so the result is not driven by the smoothing baseline. Integrated Gradients agreed with the maps (median Spearman 0.58 across brain cells; 40\% overlap of the top 10\% of cells, against 10\% expected by chance). No region differed by sex after FDR correction. By site, HH and IOP place 13--16\% less attribution density on deep grey matter and 12--15\% less on the hippocampus and amygdala than Guys ($q<0.001$); these are relative differences in density, not artefacts of region size.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
The model's own spread was larger for IOP (8.06 vs 5.66 years for Guys) and doubled when IOP was unseen (12.46 vs 6.13) with 92\% coverage, so the model signals unfamiliar data. Out of fold (Table~\ref{tab:unc}), the model's own 90\% range covered 94\% of Guys, 89\% of HH but only \textbf{77\%} of IOP subjects. Pooled split-conformal intervals, which guarantee coverage only on average, were worse for IOP (69\%), whereas per-site (Mondrian) conformal intervals covered 91--92\% at every site, at the price of much wider intervals for IOP ($\pm17.8$ vs $\pm8.2$ years for Guys). The seed ensemble's spread was also largest for IOP (3.7 vs 1.5 years). Average coverage thus hides that one site receives unreliable intervals; uncertainty must be calibrated per site, like accuracy and bias. Per subject, uncertainty tracked error only weakly (Spearman 0.16).

\section{Discussion}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{A replicated disparity that is not an age artefact.} IOP's lower brain-age gap survives every age-handling choice we could construct, appears in independent splits and in cross-validation, and is present before fine-tuning. The usual first explanation of site differences in brain age, different age distributions \cite{aguzin2026bias}, does not account for it.

\textbf{Sensitive to acquisition, but the sensitivity is not the explanation.} The model is not acquisition-invariant: an unchanged HH brain given IOP's appearance loses almost four years. Yet the explanations we can measure---intensity profile, smoothness, head size, recorded cohort characteristics, and the site signature in the features---each leave the IOP offset in place. Two methodological lessons follow. First, references for intensity and erasure must be conditioned on age, because tissue contrast and the site--age confound carry age information: unconditioned versions overstated the intensity effect and made the offset seem to vanish. Second, removing a site signature is not the same as removing a site disparity. A study that had stopped at the stress test, or at plain erasure, would have reported ``scanner bias''; the evidence here does not support that label.

\textbf{What remains.} The offset could stem from acquisition properties not modelled here (e.g., spatially varying contrast or vendor-specific reconstruction) or from cohort characteristics IXI does not record. Within IXI, site and cohort are confounded, so these cannot be separated; travelling-subject data would.

\textbf{Limitations.} IOP contributes 61 subjects in total. The cross-validated models fine-tune only the upper blocks, a lighter recipe than the main model. Quantile matching changes only the global intensity distribution, and Gaussian blur did not reproduce IOP's full sharpness profile. Attribution has 8\,mm resolution, and deletion tests are themselves affected by distribution shift. We did not evaluate a classical feature-based baseline or a second dataset. Participants are healthy volunteers; no clinical conclusion follows.

\textbf{Future work.} Travelling-subject datasets, a FreeSurfer-feature baseline (if it shows the same offset, the cause lies in the images or cohort rather than a CNN shortcut), site-adversarial fine-tuning \cite{dinsdale2021unlearning}, and passports for further models and datasets.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================
A fine-tuned SFCN is accurate on IXI and its errors are equivalent between sexes, but it shows a replicated 4--5 year brain-age offset at one site. The offset is robust to how age is handled and exists before fine-tuning. The model is clearly sensitive to acquisition appearance, yet neither that sensitivity nor any measured acquisition or cohort property explains the offset. Site disparities in brain age should be tested, not labelled: a protocol of age-robust estimates, stress tests, age-conditional erasure and graded verdicts shows what can and cannot be concluded.
