1The problem we are solving

What is "brain age"?

You have a real age (birthdays) and a biological brain age: how old your brain looks on a scan. With age the brain shrinks slightly, the fluid spaces (ventricles) grow, the surface folds (sulci) widen and the grey matter thins. An AI can learn these patterns from thousands of scans and then guess the age of a new scan.

Key termBrain-age gap = predicted age − real age. A gap of +8 means the brain looks 8 years older than the person is. Research links positive gaps with poorer health and dementia risk, which is why brain age is a popular research tool.

So what is the problem?

Most papers check only one thing: is the AI accurate? But accuracy can hide shortcuts. A student can score 95% because the answers were always in the same position, then fail when the answers are shuffled.

AIs do this too. A real, famous example: an AI that detected pneumonia on chest X-rays was partly reading which hospital's machine took the X-ray, because one hospital had more sick patients.

Our questionOur brain-age AI is accurate, but is it accurate for the right reasons? Or is it partly using which scanner took the picture? Hence the title: Right for the Wrong Reasons?

2The data

The IXI dataset

A free public dataset of brain MRI scans from about 580 healthy volunteers aged 20 to 86. After removing duplicates, missing ages and failed scans we used 485 people. We used T1 scans, the standard "anatomy" scan in which tissue types appear as different shades of grey.

SiteScannerNotes
Guys (Guy's Hospital)Philips 1.5Tthe largest group
HH (Hammersmith Hospital)Philips 3Tstronger magnet
IOP (Institute of Psychiatry)GE 1.5Tdifferent manufacturer, smallest group, recruited younger people
Key ideaEach machine gives its pictures a slightly different "look", like photos from different phone cameras: same anatomy, different brightness, contrast and sharpness. We call this an acquisition-site effect.

The label fix: an early discovery

The ages first used in the project came from a wrong source file. We rebuilt them from the official IXI spreadsheet (files marked _OLD_wrong_labels are the old ones). A good point for the review: we checked our data instead of trusting it.

Preprocessing: cleaning the scans

  1. Remove the skull, keeping only the brain.
  2. Align every brain to a standard template, so the same position means the same brain area in everyone.
  3. Crop to the same box: 160 × 192 × 160 voxels (tiny 1 mm cubes).
  4. Normalise brightness the way the original model expects.

3The model (SFCN)

SFCN ("Simple Fully Convolutional Network", Peng et al. 2021) was trained on 14,000+ brains from UK Biobank, a huge British health study, and is one of the most accurate published brain-age models.

How it works: a funnel from a 3D scan to one number

  1. Input: the whole 3D brain (160×192×160 voxels).
  2. Feature extraction (5 layers): small 3D filters slide over the brain to detect patterns. Early layers find edges and tissue boundaries; later layers combine them into complex patterns ("enlarged ventricle", "thin cortex"). After each layer the image shrinks, so the network sees bigger regions in less detail.
  3. Summary: everything is squeezed into 64 numbers, the model's internal "description" of the brain. These 64 numbers matter later (Finding ⑤).
  4. Output: not a single age but a probability for each age from 18 to 90 (72 age bins): "10% chance 60, 12% chance 61…"
  5. Final guess: the probability-weighted average of all ages.
Why probabilities?A narrow peak means the model is confident; a wide spread means it is unsure. We use this in Finding ⑦.

Fine-tuning

The original model covered only ages 42–82 (UK Biobank is middle-aged and older), but our people are 20–86. So we expanded the output to 18–90 and retrained it a little on our 339 people, like an experienced doctor learning a new hospital's machines.

VersionAverage mistake (MAE)
Original model, no retraining ("zero-shot", validation)10.2 years
After fine-tuning (final test)3.7 years

Bias correction

All brain-age models regress to the mean: they guess old people a bit too young and young people a bit too old. The standard fix (Cole's method): on the validation set, fit the line predicted = 0.83 × real + 8.3, then undo it: corrected = (predicted − 8.3) / 0.83. Without this, groups of different ages would show fake gaps.

4The audit: seven findings

Finding ①: It is accurate ✅

Test MAE 3.71 years (95% CI 3.10–4.38) and correlation with real age r = 0.97 (1.0 would be perfect), comparable to published results.

What is a confidence interval (CI)?We have only 73 test people; different people would give a slightly different number. The CI is the range the true value very likely lies in. We computed it by bootstrapping: re-sampling our people 5,000 times and seeing how much the result varies. If a CI for a difference includes 0, there is no clear effect.

Finding ②: Fair by sex, not fair by site

We compared brain-age gaps between groups while controlling for age: IOP recruited younger people and gaps change with age, so without this control we would wrongly blame the scanner.

ComparisonGap difference95% CIMeaning
Male vs female−0.6 yrs−2.7 to +1.6includes 0 → no real difference ✅

IOP brains are predicted about 5 years younger than they should be. It appears in both validation (−4.1) and test (−5.1), so it is not a fluke. We call it an acquisition-site effect, never "hospital bias": we have not shown that the hospital did anything wrong.

Finding ③: Leave-one-site-out ❌

We trained the model without one hospital and tested it on that hospital, simulating "a new hospital starts using our AI".

Left outExtra error on that siteMeaning
IOP+3.3 yrs (CI 1.6–4.9)real damage: IOP scans are genuinely "different" to the model
HH+0.25 yrs (CI −0.9–1.4)fine

Finding ④: The heat maps (where does it look?) ✅

A Grad-CAM-style heat map (HiResCAM variant) answers: which parts of the brain pushed the prediction most? Our maps highlight the ventricles, deep grey matter and the surrounding regions, which are biologically sensible ageing areas.

But heat maps can lie: some methods produce pretty pictures regardless of what the model learned. So we ran two honesty tests:

  1. Sanity check (randomisation): scramble the model's learned weights and recompute the maps. If the maps barely change, they were fake. Ours fell apart (similarity ρ = 0.13), so they reflect what the model learned.
  2. Deletion test: blur away the regions the map ranks highest and see how much the age changes, compared with random and least-important regions.
Blurred 5% of the brainAverage age change
Most important (by map)4.8 years
Random regions1.0 years
Least important0.5 years

The map really points at what drives the prediction (p = 5×10⁻¹¹ across 60 brains). Maps differed only slightly between groups, so the model looks at roughly the same places for everyone.

Finding ⑤: The model learned the scanner ❌ (the heart of the project)

Test A: the site probe (the "detective"). We took the 64 internal numbers for each brain and trained a simple classifier to guess which hospital took the scan from those numbers alone. The model was only ever taught age; nobody gave it hospital information.

What the detective seesCorrect guesses (balanced)
Just age and sex41% (≈ chance, which is 33%)
The model's 64 numbers93%
64 numbers with age/sex information removed91%
MeaningThe hospital fingerprint is inside the model's internal description. It is not explained by different ages: removing age and sex barely changes it.

Test B: the scanner-look swap (stress test). We took one brain and changed only its look: its brightness and contrast profile was re-mapped to another hospital's typical profile, with the anatomy untouched. The comparison is fair: against the same brain given its own hospital's look. The hospital "looks" were built from training people only, adjusted to the same age and sex mix.

SwapAge change
HH brain → IOP look−3.9 years
IOP brain → HH look+3.1 years
Guys → IOP / IOP → Guys+0.1 / +0.9 years
MeaningThe change went the same way in 100% of brains. Changing only the picture's look changes the age. That is "right for the wrong reasons", shown directly.

Finding ⑥: But what exactly causes IOP's −5 years? ⚠️

We tried to explain IOP's offset and ruled things out one by one, like a detective:

  1. Brightness/contrast? It shifts IOP by about 1 year at most compared with its own look, which is not enough to explain 5.
  2. Blur? All sites have the same native resolution, but IOP scans are measurably blurrier (≈0.9 mm wider blur). We blurred Guys and HH scans to IOP's level and they did not get younger (+0.9 and 0.0 years). Ruled out.
  3. ComBat harmonisation (a standard "scanner eraser" that removes site differences from the 64 numbers). The detective dropped from 91% → 36% (almost chance): the fingerprint is erased. Accuracy stayed fine (MAE 3.81 → 3.90). But IOP's gap barely moved: −4.5 → −4.0.
ConclusionThe scanner fingerprint is real (Finding ⑤), but it does not fully explain IOP's younger brains. The rest may come from who IOP recruited, a different group of volunteers. In IXI, site and population are tangled together, so we cannot separate them. Saying so honestly is a strength: many papers would simply claim "scanner bias" without testing it.

Finding ⑦: It knows when it is unsure ✅

Using the width of the age-probability peak:

  • On IOP brains the model is less sure: spread 8.1 years vs 5.7 for Guys.
  • The model trained without IOP was twice as unsure on IOP (12.5 vs 6.1).
  • Its "90% range" contained the true age 88–92% of the time, so its stated confidence is honest.
MeaningThe AI raises a flag when something is unfamiliar. That is a useful safety feature: on a new site, check its uncertainty first.

5Putting it together: the verdict

The model is accurate and fair between men and women. But it has learned the scanner's fingerprint, and changing only a scan's look shifts its age by 3–4 years. IOP brains come out about 5 years younger, partly due to acquisition effects and partly something we cannot separate (probably the population). Its heat maps and its uncertainty are honest. So: right on average, but partly for the wrong reasons. Don't compare brain-age gaps across scanners without checking first.

What makes it different

Most projects stop at "accuracy is X, here are some heat maps". We went further: we tested the heat maps instead of trusting them, proved a shortcut exists (detective + look swap), ruled out explanations one by one, checked that the AI knows when it is unsure, and packaged it all as a Fairness Passport and a live demo.

7The seven rules and why each exists

RuleWhy
1. One model trained on everyoneseparate models per group would hide the very problem we study
2. "Acquisition-site effects", not "hospital bias"we cannot prove the hospital caused anything; site is tangled with population
3. Never "the model thinks differently"we describe measurable behaviour, not thoughts
4. No clinical claimshealthy volunteers only; research use
5. Control for agesites recruited different ages; ignoring it creates fake gaps
6. Report confidence intervalssmall groups make single numbers unreliable
7. Never tune on the test setotherwise the final score is fake

8Honest limitations and future work

Say these before anyone else does
  • Small groups: only 9 IOP people in the test set (but the effect repeats in validation).
  • One random split and one training run (one seed).
  • Site and population are tangled in IXI, so the cause of IOP's offset cannot be fully separated.
  • Healthy adults aged 20–86 only.
  • Heat maps are coarse (blocks of about 8 mm).
  • Uncertainty is the model's own spread, not a formally calibrated interval.

Future work: more sites and datasets, especially "travelling subjects" (the same people scanned on several machines, which separates scanner from population); training the model to ignore site (domain-adversarial training); several seeds and splits.

9Check your understanding

If you can explain these three out loud, you understand the project:

  1. Why does the detective's 91% prove a problem, if the model was only taught age?
    Because information it was never asked to learn (the site) is still clearly encoded in its internal description, even after age and sex are removed, so the model carries a scanner fingerprint.
  2. Why didn't ComBat fix IOP's −5 years, and what does that tell us?
    ComBat erased the fingerprint (91% → 36%) but the IOP gap only went from −4.5 to −4.0. So the fingerprint is not the whole story; part of the offset is something else, probably who IOP recruited, which IXI cannot separate.
  3. Why do we trust the heat maps?
    Two tests: scrambling the model destroys them (ρ 0.13), and blurring the regions they highlight changes the age 4–5 times more than blurring random regions.

Likely review questions

Why not just report accuracy?Accuracy hides shortcuts; our model is accurate yet uses scanner fingerprints.
Why adjust for age?IOP's volunteers were younger and gaps depend on age; otherwise we would blame the scanner for an age effect.
Only 9 IOP test subjects: too few?A limitation, but the effect repeats in validation (−4.1) and test (−5.1) and the CIs exclude 0.
Can this be used in hospitals?No. Research only, healthy volunteers. The passport says so explicitly.
What next?More scanners, travelling-subject data, site-invariant training, multiple seeds.
